So Delicious recall: the same stones, the same pint, nine months apart

FDA Foreign material Analysis Published September 21, 2026

On September 15, 2026, Danone USA recalled So Delicious Dairy Free® Salted Caramel Cluster non-dairy frozen dessert pints because they may contain “small stones and other hard objects” in the cashew inclusions. It is an ordinary foreign-material recall, and it would not be worth an article — except that the FDA's own site carries a second notice for the same product, with the same UPC, for the same stones, dated December 15, 2025.

The September 2026 recall, in numbers

Company: Danone USA (White Plains, NY and Louisville, CO). Product: So Delicious Dairy Free® Salted Caramel Cluster Non-Dairy Frozen Dessert, pints. UPC: 744473476138. SKU: 136603. Best-by dates: on and before April 3, 2028. Hazard: small stones and other hard objects within the cashew inclusions. Distribution: retail stores nationwide. Discovered: through consumer complaints. Consumer line: 1-833-367-8975, Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. ET.

Two So Delicious notices, one unchanged defect

Set the two announcements side by side and the overlap is almost total. Same brand, same flavour, same pint format, same UPC 744473476138, same SKU 136603. Same stated hazard, in nearly the same words: stones and hard objects in the cashew pieces. The December 2025 notice covered best-by dates before August 8, 2027; the September 2026 notice covers dates on and before April 3, 2028.

Those date ranges are the tell. A best-by date on a frozen dessert sits well over a year past production, so the second range reaches product made months after the first recall closed. This is not one contaminated batch found twice. It is the same defect reappearing in later production.

Both times, the FDA notice says the problem surfaced through consumer complaints. Nobody caught it in the plant, in a supplier audit, or in finished-product inspection — not the first time, and not the second time either.

Why cashews, and why stones

Stones in nuts are not exotic. Cashews are dried and sorted on the ground or on hard floors in the producing country, and small stones of similar size and density travel with the kernels. Optical sorters and density separators remove most of them; the residue is what quality control is for.

An inclusion — the cluster, swirl or chunk stirred into a frozen dessert — is the hardest place to catch that residue. The nut arrives already processed, often already coated in caramel, and it is added late in the line, after the steps where metal detection and screening would normally sit. A stone inside a caramel-coated cashew is invisible to the eye, passes a metal detector without a flicker, and is discovered by the only remaining inspector in the chain: a tooth.

That is why a repeat matters more than a first occurrence. A first recall says a batch went wrong. A second, nine months later, with the same ingredient and the same failure mode, points at something upstream that was not changed — a supplier, a specification, or an inspection step that does not exist.

What a recall notice does not have to tell you

Neither announcement states how many units were involved, whether anyone was injured, or whether the cashews came from the same supplier both times. None of that is an omission in the legal sense: an FDA recall press release is a public-warning document, not an investigation report. Companies are required to identify the product and the hazard, and they routinely stop there.

It does mean the public record cannot answer the question that matters most — what changed after December 2025 — and that the only visible evidence either way is whether a third notice appears.

There is also no injury count, which is worth reading carefully. “No injuries reported” is not in either notice; the notices are simply silent. Silence is not a clean bill of health. Chipped teeth and dental work rarely get reported to a manufacturer, and almost never to the FDA.

What to do

Check your freezer for So Delicious Dairy Free® Salted Caramel Cluster pints, UPC 744473476138. If the best-by date is on or before April 3, 2028, do not eat it. Danone is offering a refund or replacement coupon through the consumer line at 1-833-367-8975. No other So Delicious flavours or products are affected. Because the best-by dates run far into the future, do not assume a pint is too old to be covered — with this recall, a later date is what puts you in scope, not an earlier one.
